Knowing when you have enough spectra

The statistics that guide whether to continue collecting, move to the next sample, or pause to investigate an issue.

A sample is measured by running the same energy scan several times and averaging the results. Averaging n repetitions reduces random noise by √(n), so before every additional repetition there is a decision to make: is the next scan worth the beam time it costs?

Three separate conditions need to be checked:

  1. the repetitions are repetitions of the same thing – the sample has not changed under the beam, and beamline and sample are still in proper position;
  2. the residual dispersion is still photon-limited, so that averaging is still buying something;
  3. the uncertainty on the merged spectrum has reached the level the science requires.

Conditions 1 and 2 are the ones that are easy to miss, because a measurement that fails either of them still looks like it is converging. The rest of this document builds up the quantities needed to test all three.

Every figure below is produced by running the analysis described here on four simulated scan series with known defects. Series A is a clean, photon-limited measurement; series B suffers a positional disturbance partway through; series C is progressively photoreduced; series D is bumped once, late. Every plotted number is the output of the implementation cited in the footnote, not an illustration drawn by hand.

1The data

One scan repetition is one pass of the monochromator across the energy range, recording detector counts at each energy point. A series of n reps on one sample position gives a stack of n spectra measured on a common energy grid.

What follows is written for photon-counting fluorescence detection. Total fluorescence yield and high-energy-resolution fluorescence detection9 produce the same object – a stack of spectra on a shared energy axis – and both give a counted number of photons per point, which is what the counting-statistics floor of §5 is built from. Transmission is outside this scope: there the absorption is μ = ln(I0/I1), whose counting error propagates differently and diverges as μ → 0. So is EXAFS, whose noise is judged in k or R space and not as a per-point relative error on μ(E).

Each rep is edge-step normalised before anything else happens: the pre-edge background is subtracted and the spectrum is scaled so that the pre-edge region sits at 0 and the post-edge region at 1, making the absorption edge exactly one unit tall.1 This allows us to directly compare reps taken at different sample thicknesses or concentrations, so the stack can be averaged.

2The feature window

Convergence is considered not as a property of a whole spectrum, but as a property of a specific feature within the spectrum – by default the white line, but can be configured to another feature of interest. The feature window [Emin, Emax] is the energy range bracketing that feature.

The window should start a few eV below where the feature begins to rise and to end a few eV above where it returns to the local baseline, isolating the part of the spectrum that carries information.

The window has two jobs, and they disagree at its edges. It has to bracket the whole feature, shoulders included, because the per-rep value of §7 is usually an integral, and an integral truncated on the flanks of a peak is not that peak's area. It also serves as the averaging domain for a relative error, which needs a denominator that is not near zero – and on the shoulders the signal is at its weakest, so a relative error computed there is large for reasons that have nothing to do with the quality of the measurement. Narrowing the window would fix the statistics by mismeasuring the feature. Keeping the shoulders and excluding them from the averages fixes the statistics instead: the outermost 5% of points at each end, at least one point per end, are trimmed.

3The merged spectrum and its standard error

The merge of the first n reps is their point-by-point mean:

merged spectrum μn(E)  =  (1/n)  ∑i=1..n  yi(E)

where yi(E) is rep i at energy E. This is the spectrum that gets published. The question is how well it is known.

Standard deviation versus standard error

These are different quantities and the distinction is the core of everything below.

The sample standard deviation sn(E) measures how much the individual reps disagree with one another at energy E:

dispersion between reps, at one energy sn(E)2  =  (1/(n−1))  ∑i=1..n  [ yi(E) − μn(E) ]2

The divisor n−1 rather than n is Bessel's correction. An estimator is biased if its average value over many repeats of the experiment differs from the quantity it is estimating; unlike noise, bias does not average away. The deviations summed above are measured from the sample mean rather than from the true mean, and the sample mean sits closer to the data than the true mean does, so every squared deviation comes out systematically too small. Dividing by n−1 rather than n scales the sum back up by exactly the right amount and makes s2 an unbiased estimate of the true variance σ2. It also means sn is undefined at n = 1: a single scan contains no information about disagreement between scans.

sn does not shrink as reps accumulate. It is a property of the measurement, and its expected value is the same whether two reps or twenty have been taken. What shrinks is the uncertainty on the average – the standard error of the mean, or SEM:

standard error of the merge, at one energy SEMn(E)  =  sn(E)  /  [ c4(n) · √(n) ]

The √(n) is the reason repeating scans works at all: averaging n independent measurements of the same quantity produces an estimate that is √(n) times tighter than any one of them. The factor c4(n) corrects a small-sample bias in sn and is derived in §4.

{{FIG3}}

Figure 3. The merge of the first 2, 4 and 12 reps of series A over the feature window, each with its ±SEM band. The centre line barely moves; the band tightens by a factor of √(6) ≈ 2.4 between n = 2 and n = 12.

Reducing the window to a single number

SEMn(E) is a curve, one value per energy point, in the same units as the spectrum. To compare it against a target, against other samples, and against the physical limit defined in §5, it is converted to a relative error at each point – divided by the local signal μn(E) – and averaged over the trimmed window:

the convergence metric SEM(n)  =  100% ×  ⟨ sn(E)  /  [ c4(n) · √(n) · μn(E) ] ⟩E ∈ window

⟨ · ⟩E denotes the mean over the trimmed feature window. SEM(n) is a percentage, and it is undefined at n = 1.

Edge-step normalisation has already made samples of different thickness and concentration comparable, so that is not what the division does. What it does is express the uncertainty as a fraction of the feature it is measured on, so that a 1% target means the same thing for a small pre-edge peak as for a tall white line. It is also why the window matters so much. Outside the window, μn(E) → 0 in the pre-edge region, and a ratio with a vanishing denominator would dominate the average while carrying no information.

Figure 4. How a stack becomes one number, for series A at n = 12. (a) The merge, with error bars showing ±s12(E) – the disagreement between the twelve thin traces behind it. (b) The same quantity divided by √(12)·c4 and by the local signal, giving a relative error at each energy. Its average over the untrimmed points is SEM(12) = 0.92%.

4The small-sample correction c4(n)

Bessel's correction removes the bias in s2 (the sample standard deviation squared). A second bias survives it, because averaging and taking a square root do not commute. The square root is concave, so the average of √(s2) falls below √(average of s2) – Jensen's inequality. So s is a biased estimate of σ even though s2 is an unbiased estimate of σ2 (the variance). For normally distributed data the shortfall has a closed form:2

expected value of the sample standard deviation E[s]  =  c4(n) · σ    where    c4(n)  =  √(2/(n−1))  ·  Γ(n/2) / Γ((n−1)/2)

Γ is the gamma function, the continuous extension of the factorial: Γ(k) = (k−1)! for integer k. The name c4 is the label this constant carries in the statistical process-control literature, where it is tabulated rather than computed.3

The bias is severe exactly where scan series live. The sample standard deviation reads 20% low at n = 2, 8% low at n = 4, and is still 2% low at n = 14. Dividing by c4(n) removes it. The same small-sample problem is recognised in the IXAS committee's recommendations, which handle it by tabulating confidence bounds on s/σ rather than by correcting the bias: at four scans they give 0.27σ ≤ s ≤ 1.76σ, which is worth keeping in view alongside the point correction, since it is the spread of the estimate rather than its offset.11 The correction is applied wherever a standard deviation is divided by √(n) in what follows, both to the spectrum-level curve SEM(n) of §3 and to the running standard error of the per-rep feature value in §7. It cancels out of the projection in §9, for the reason given there.

{{FIG5}}

Figure 5. (a) c4(n) against rep count. (b) The consequence for series A. The uncorrected curve is biased low by a known, reproducible amount – 20% at n = 2, 11% at n = 3 – which is enough to put the opening points of the curve underneath the counting-statistics floor of §5 and invite exactly the wrong reading of the plot. Dividing by c4(n) removes the offset and the curve sits on the floor, where a clean photon-limited measurement belongs.

5The counting-statistics floor

X-ray detection is photon counting. Photons arrive independently, so the number recorded in a fixed interval follows a Poisson distribution, whose defining property is that its variance equals its mean. Recording N photons therefore carries a standard deviation of √(N) and a relative error of

poisson relative error √(N) / N  =  1 / √(N)

Nothing in the analysis can improve on this. It is fixed by how many photons were recorded, and it is the reason count rate and integration time – not repetition count – are the levers that change what is achievable.4

Merging n reps multiplies the total recorded photons by n, so the achievable relative error on the merge falls as 1/√(n). Averaged over the same trimmed window:

counting-statistics floor at n reps floor(n)  =  100% ×  ⟨ 1 / √(N(E)) ⟩E ∈ window  /  √(n)

N(E) is the mean per-rep detector counts at energy E – the raw counts, before normalisation, summed across the reps collected and divided by their number.

The decisive property of floor(n) is that it is computed from the recorded counts and not from the dispersion between reps. It is an independent, absolute reference. There are three cases to consider for the comparison:

This comparison is the community's recommendation. The International XAFS Society's Standards and Criteria Committee proposed in 2000 that the measured scan-to-scan dispersion and the counting-statistics expectation both be computed, and then compared against each other.11 Its Eq. (9) is SEMn(E) as defined in §3, with the same insistence that the 1/[M(M−1)] prefactor be used “to ensure that the results of this estimate be representative of the uncertainty in ⟨χ⟩ (as opposed to the spread within the set of measurements)”. Its Eqs. (7)–(8) are floor(n), computed from “the actual number of photon counts” – for fluorescence, up to an incident-flux term 1/N0 that is negligible while N0 ≫ Nf. And it states that “only then will the estimate from Eq. (9) be directly comparable to those obtained with Eqs. (5-8)”. The recommendation is a reporting standard, applied once to a finished dataset; what follows applies it after every rep.

The same committee notes that XAFS noise is usually not limited by photon statistics, being dominated instead by systematic measurement error. That is not in tension with anything here. The floor is used as a reference to measure against, not as a level anyone expects to reach, which is why §6 tests the ratio against its own opening trend rather than against unity.

  • SEM(n) tracking floor(n) – the only thing limiting the measurement is the number of photons collected. More reps will help, as 1/√(n).
  • SEM(n) sitting above floor(n) – the dispersion between reps exceeds what photon statistics can account for. Something other than counting noise is contributing.
  • SEM(n) persistently well below floor(n) – an input is wrong: either the SEM or the floor has been miscalculated. Note the word persistently: sn is itself an estimate from a handful of reps. So at small n, individual points below 1 are possible. A whole series sitting below the floor is not.
What the comparison assumes. floor(n) is a relative error on the raw counts, while SEM(n) is a relative error on the edge-step normalised spectrum. Normalisation is affine, so propagating counting error through it gives a relative error on the normalised spectrum of [1 + b(E)/(Δμ·μn(E))]/√(N), where b(E) is the subtracted pre-edge background and Δμ the edge step. The bracket is always at least 1, so floor(n) as computed is a slight under-estimate of the true achievable error – a genuine lower bound, which is what a floor should be, and the reason a ratio sitting below 1 across a whole series indicates a mismatched input.

Figure 6. Series A – a photon-limited measurement. (a) On log–log axes the 1/√(n) floor is a straight line of slope −½, and the measured SEM lies on it. (b) The same information as the ratio SEM(n)/floor(n), which stays between 0.95 and 1.05 at every rep count – variation about 1, not a trend away from it. 6Departure from the floor

In some cases the evolution of the SEM can break off a trendline and form a plateau. The scan repetition number at which it begins to diverge is significant for understanding which scan reps are usable in data analysis. It is located as follows. Let r(n) = SEM(n)/floor(n). A baseline r0 – the opening trend – is taken as the median of the first three defined values of r, before something plausibly would have gone wrong. The onset is the smallest n for which

departure criterion r(n) > 1.25 r0   and   median{ r(m) : m ≥ n } > 1.25 r0

The second condition is what distinguishes a systematic from an outlier. A single anomalous rep lifts r at one point and the series returns; a real change in the measurement lifts r and it stays lifted. Requiring the median of everything from n onward to remain above the threshold selects only the second case. Locating the onset rather than merely noting that the series as a whole underperforms is the point of the exercise: the reps before the onset are still good data.

{{FIG7}}

Figure 7. Series B – the sample position was disturbed after rep 5. The ratio opens at 1.3 and settles to about 1.1 over reps 3–5; from rep 6 it jumps to 2.0 and climbs to 2.5, staying above 1.9 for the remaining nine reps. That is a sustained departure rather than a fluctuation, so it is flagged as a departure at rep 6. Reps 1–5 are a clean measurement; reps 6–14 are a measurement of something else.

What a departure does and does not mean

A possibly counter-intuitive note: SEM(n) continues to fall as 1/√(n) after the departure – visibly so in Figure 7. Additional reps do keep making the merged spectrum more precise. But the ability to interpret the merged spectrum could be lost.

Two situations produce a departure, and the ratio test cannot tell them apart:

  • Excess random dispersion – unstable incident flux, a beam spot wandering over a heterogeneous sample. The merge remains an estimate of a single quantity, but each rep is buying less than it should. The correct response is to fix the instability, not to pay √(n) for it.
  • A change in the sample or the instrument – a step in the optics, a spot move, beam damage. The merge is now an average over two or more distinct states, and it converges to a weighted mean that corresponds to neither of them.

In both cases the average spectra have stopped being photon-limited, which is enough to justify stopping and diagnosing. Distinguishing them is the job of §7.

7Testing whether the sample changed

A series is stationary if the quantity being measured is preserved while it is measured, so that the reps vary about a fixed value. It is drifting if that quantity is systematically moving. At a beamline the usual causes are photoreduction or other beam damage pulling the white line down, the beam spot walking off the sample, monochromator energy calibration creeping (it is important to derive the energy axis using the monochromator encoder to avoid this), or changes in incident flux or sample temperature.5

This is the failure mode a precision test structurally cannot see. Averaging assumes every rep samples the same underlying quantity. If it is moving, SEM still falls as 1/√(n), and the analysis reports an ever more precise estimate of a number that corresponds to no actual state of the sample.

The per-rep feature value

The test operates not on the spectra but on a scalar summary of each rep. Each rep is reduced to one number xi over the same feature window – most usefully the integral of the window (peak area), but also its maximum (white-line height), its argmax (peak position), or its range (prominence above the local baseline). The result is a trace x1, …, xn in collection order, and the question becomes whether that trace has a trend.

The integral is generally the right choice for a white line. The maximum is an order statistic: it is upward-biased, and it deliberately selects the noisiest single point in the window, inflating rep-to-rep dispersion for no gain in sensitivity.

Mann–Kendall: is there a trend?

For every pair of reps i < j, record only whether the later value is higher or lower than the earlier one. A pair is concordant if the later value is higher and discordant if it is lower. Kendall's rank correlation coefficient τ is the normalised difference between the two counts:6

kendall rank correlation τ  =  (C − D) / [ n(n−1)/2 ]      τ ∈ [−1, +1]

C and D are the numbers of concordant and discordant pairs and n(n−1)/2 is the total number of pairs. τ = +1 means every rep exceeded every rep before it; τ = −1 means strictly falling; τ = 0 means no monotone tendency.

The associated p-value is the probability of observing a τ at least this far from zero if the reps had been in random order. A small p-value means the ordering is unlikely to be an accident. The null distribution needed to turn τ into a probability comes from the orderings themselves: if rep order carries no information then all n! permutations are equally likely, and the fraction of them reaching a given |τ| can be counted exactly. At the rep counts of interest here that exact enumeration is what is used. For larger n it is replaced by a normal approximation, in which the concordance excess C − D has zero mean and variance n(n−1)(2n+5)/18 under the null. The test is distribution-free: it assumes nothing about the shape of the noise and does not require the drift to be linear. Because it uses only the direction of each pairwise comparison and not the magnitude, one wild rep cannot manufacture a trend. It needs at least five reps to have any power at the default α: with four reps the most extreme ordering attainable has a two-sided p of 0.083, so no four-rep series can clear p < 0.05. Below five the correct output is unknown, not no trend.

Theil–Sen: how large is it?

Mann–Kendall reports whether a trend is real, not whether it matters. The magnitude comes from the Theil–Sen estimator, the median of the slopes of all lines through pairs of points:7

robust slope, per rep slope  =  mediani < j  ( xj − xi ) / ( j − i )

Reported as a fraction of the mean, and multiplied by n−1, it gives the total excursion: how far the feature value moved across the whole series, as a percentage.

The estimator is chosen for its breakdown point – the fraction of the data that can be arbitrarily corrupted before the estimate becomes meaningless. For Theil–Sen it is about 29%: roughly one rep in three can be garbage. Ordinary least squares has a breakdown point of zero; a single outlier drags the fitted slope arbitrarily far.8

Why both are required

Significance without magnitude is a trap in the other direction. Given enough reps, Mann–Kendall resolves arbitrarily small trends, so a real but scientifically irrelevant 0.1% drift would eventually block a perfectly good merge. The series is therefore called drifting only when the trend is both significant and material:

drift criterion drifting  ⇔  ( p < 0.05 )  and  ( total excursion > 1% of the mean )

The 1% is deliberately the same scale as the SEM target: a trend smaller than the noise level being demanded is not a trend worth stopping for.

{{FIG8}}

Figure 8. Per-rep white-line area, as a percentage of the series mean, with the Theil–Sen line. (a) Series A varies about a fixed value; τ = −0.06 is not significant (p = 0.84) and the implied excursion is 0.23%, below the materiality threshold. (b) Series C is being photoreduced: τ = −0.55, p = 0.014, total excursion 2.1%. Both conditions are met, so the series is called drifting.

The complementarity is the point. Series C tracks the counting floor for its whole length – SEM(n)/floor(n) stays between 1.05 and 1.2, never approaching the 25% departure threshold, and the merge meets its 1% target at rep 12. On the §5 test alone it is a textbook photon-limited measurement. It is also a sample that lost 2% of its white-line area to the beam while it was being measured. Conversely, series B is caught cleanly by the floor test (Figure 7) and passes the drift test with p = 0.52, because a single step change is not a monotone trend. Neither test subsumes the other.

The rising-SEM veto

One check requires no statistical test at all. For genuine repetitions of one measurement the running standard error can only fall: s converges to a fixed σ while the divisor √(n) grows. If the running SEM grows instead, σ is being re-estimated upward faster than √(n) shrinks it, which happens only when later reps disagree with earlier ones.

It is treated as an automatic veto on a converged verdict. The test compares the last value of the running SEM against the smallest value in the final third of the series, requiring at least eight reps and a rise of more than 50% before firing – comparing against a window minimum rather than the immediately preceding point so that one noisy rep does not trip it.

The threshold has to be set against the sampling noise of the statistic it tests, which is why it is looser than the other margins here. The running standard error of the feature value is estimated from a single number per rep, so it carries n − 1 degrees of freedom and a relative standard deviation near 1/√(2(n−1)) – 38% at n = 4, 27% at n = 8. A 25% margin sits inside the noise of its own statistic, and taking the minimum over the final third as the denominator biases the ratio upward besides. Two standard deviations of the statistic is 1 + 2/√(2(n−1)), which runs from 1.53 at n = 8 to 1.37 at n = 16; 50% is the single fixed value closest to that band over the useful range.

8Two further checks on the stack

Between-position heterogeneity

When reps come from more than one position on the sample, the stack may be averaging over genuinely different material rather than repeating one measurement. A one-way analysis of variance separates the two. At each energy point, the variance between position means is compared against the variance within positions:

heterogeneity f-statistic, at one energy F(E)  =  [ ∑k nk (μk(E) − μ(E))2 / (K−1) ]  /  [ ∑k ∑i (yki(E) − μk(E))2 / (n − K) ]

K is the number of positions, nk the rep count at position k, μk its mean spectrum, and μ the rep-weighted grand mean. The reported statistic is F(E) averaged over the window.

F ≈ 1 means positions differ by no more than reps at one position do, and the stack can be averaged. F > 5 means they differ well beyond shot noise: the combined average is a population mean over distinct chemistries, not the spectrum of one, and no number of additional reps will converge it to a single answer. Because each rep was edge-step normalised, differences in thickness or concentration between positions have already been absorbed – what remains is a difference in spectral shape.

Screening single reps

A single bad rep in an otherwise clean stack escapes every test so far. The trend test uses only the direction of pairwise comparisons, so one wild rep cannot move it; the floor comparison absorbs that rep into a dispersion averaged over the whole stack; and the veto reads the shape of the running error rather than individual reps. Identifying one rep as inconsistent with the others needs a comparison against the counting error expected at each point, which §5 has already computed.

leave-one-out reduced chi-square, per rep χ2i  =  (1/m)  ∑E ∈ window  [ yi(E) − μ−i(E) ]2  /  σfloor(E)2

μ−i is the merge of every rep except i, m the number of retained window points, and σfloor(E) = √(Ni(E))/Δμ the counting error at energy E in the units of the normalised spectrum, with Δμ the edge step in counts. Leaving rep i out of the mean it is measured against removes the correlation between a rep and its own contribution to that mean.

The expected value is 1 for a rep whose disagreement with the others is pure counting noise, so the scale carries meaning without a calibration step. Series A returns a median of 0.94 over its twelve reps, and its spread of 0.67 to 1.48 is what 33 retained window points imply, the reduced statistic having a standard deviation of √(2/m) = 0.25. Series C, which is being photoreduced, sits a little higher at a median of 1.17 and puts no single rep forward: progressive damage is a trend, and §7 owns it.

Series B is the case the screen exists for. Its first five reps return 0.87 to 1.39, at the counting limit. Rep 6 returns 15.4, and the reps after it stay between 1.9 and 15.8. A statistic that never looks at the series as a whole locates the disturbance to the rep, and agrees with the departure onset that §6 finds from an entirely different quantity.

A rep above 3 is flagged for inspection. That threshold comes from the observed spread rather than from the χ2 distribution: across the three series without a step change the largest single value is 1.59, so 3 leaves room for the dispersion of the estimate while still catching eight of the nine disturbed reps in series B. What it does not catch is a single late disturbance whose rep is only mildly inconsistent – series D's rep 10 is the largest in its series at 1.44, which is inside the clean spread. The rising-SEM veto is what covers that case.

One property matters for reading the output. Each rep is measured against the merge of the others, so what the statistic finds is the side of a split that is in the minority. When a disturbance takes over most of the stack, the reps it flags are the clean ones. It locates the split and does not say which side of it is the good measurement; the departure onset of §6 is what settles that.

9Projecting the cost of finishing

When a series is stationary, photon-limited, and still short of its target, the remaining question is how much beam time the target costs. SEM falls as k/√(n), so the total rep count needed to reach a target T scales as the square of how far away it currently is:

projected total reps to reach the target nrequired  =  ⌈ n · ( SEM(n) / T )2 ⌉

⌈ · ⌉ is the ceiling: round up to the next whole rep.

No c4 appears here, and its absence is worth a line, because §4 asked for the correction wherever a standard deviation is divided by √(n). The target is tested against the reported SEM, which carries 1/c4 at the projected rep count as well as at the current one, so the two corrections divide out. Written exactly, the requirement is the smallest m for which c4(m)√(m) ≥ c4(n)√(n) · SEM(n)/T, and for n ≳ 5 that agrees with the expression above to within a rep. Below five reps it does not: at n = 2 the simple form over-predicts by three reps, because SEM(2) is inflated by 1/c4(2) = 1.25 while the rep count it is being projected onto is not. The projection is not worth quoting before about five reps.

Halving the remaining error costs four times the beam time. A series at 1.4% with a 1% target needs twice its current rep count; the same series with a 0.5% target needs eight times.

{{FIG10}}

Figure 10. Series A, projected forward from rep 5. At that point SEM = 1.39%, giving nrequired = ⌈5 × (1.39/1.00)2⌉ = 10. The series in fact met the target at rep 10.

This number, not the current SEM, is what should be weighed against the remaining time. If ten reps are needed and there is time for four, the available levers are longer integration per point, fewer filters or a higher incident flux, or an explicitly relaxed target – not more reps of the same length, which is the one option the arithmetic has already ruled out.

10Conclusion

After each scan, there are 3 questions to answer. Questions 1 and 2 identify the cases in which additional data is worthless. Question 3 resolves whether we have the required precision.

QuestionCriterionIf yes
1. Is it still the same sample? Mann–Kendall p < 0.05 and Theil–Sen total excursion > 1%; or the running SEM has risen by more than 50% off its recent minimum Stop and diagnose. More reps sharpen a moving target. Check for beam damage and spot drift.
2. Is it still photon-limited? SEM(n)/floor(n) has risen 25% above its opening trend and stayed there – forming a plateau on the SEM trend Stop and diagnose. The dispersion exceeds what photon statistics can account for. The reps before the onset are the clean data.
3. Is the precision sufficient? SEM(n) ≤ the target (1% by default) Stop. The requested statistics have been achieved; further reps buy precision nothing downstream can use.

If none of the above holds – the series is on the floor, stationary and still above target – collection continues. Reps still buy signal-to-noise as 1/√(n), and nrequired is what to compare against the time remaining.

A fifth outcome sits outside the sequence: if the positions being averaged are chemically heterogeneous (F > 5), the series has no single answer to converge to, and the choice is between reporting a population average and analysing the positions separately.

12Parameters and their defaults

How these defaults were chosen

The values below come from three different kinds of reasoning, and it is worth being explicit about which, because they do not carry equal weight.

A few follow from the arithmetic and cannot sensibly be anything else. The minimum of two reps is where a dispersion estimate first exists; five is where the trend criterion can first reach p < 0.05; eight is where the rising-SEM comparison has a window to work in. Others are set by tying one number to another already chosen: the trend materiality threshold is the same 1% as the SEM target on purpose, since a drift smaller than the precision being demanded is not a drift worth stopping for. The rising-SEM factor is set against the sampling noise of the statistic it tests, as §7 describes.

The rest – the SEM target itself, the departure margin, the window trim, the heterogeneity bands, the screening threshold – are working values. They were chosen to behave sensibly on the four reference series used throughout this document and on the scans that motivated the analysis, which is enough to show that each test responds to the defect it was built for. It is not enough to fix the value. Establishing how large a defect has to be before a test notices it, and how often a test fires when nothing is wrong, needs many more series than four, with known ground truth – which in practice means a retrospective pass over archived multi-rep beamtime and a period of running the analysis alongside human judgement in the field.

Until that has been done, treat the second group as starting points. They are recorded here, in one table, rather than buried in the code precisely so that changing one is a deliberate act with a visible history.
