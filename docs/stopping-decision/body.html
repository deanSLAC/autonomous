Deciding when a scan series is finished

The statistics that separate we have enough data from more data will not help, for repeated X-ray absorption scans.

A sample is measured by running the same energy scan several times and averaging the results. Averaging n repetitions reduces random noise by √(n), so before every additional repetition there is a decision to make: is the next scan worth the beam time it costs? This document sets out the calculation that answers that question – what is computed, from what, and in what order the results have to be read.

Three separate conditions have to hold before the answer is no more are needed, and no single statistic tests all three:

  1. the repetitions are repetitions of the same thing – the sample has not changed under the beam, and the instrument has not moved;
  2. the residual dispersion is still photon-limited, so that averaging is still buying something;
  3. the uncertainty on the merged spectrum has reached the level the science requires.

Conditions 1 and 2 are the ones that are easy to miss, because a measurement that fails either of them still looks like it is converging. The rest of this document builds up the quantities needed to test all three, then states the order in which they must be evaluated.

Every figure below is produced by running the analysis described here on four simulated scan series with known defects. Series A is a clean, photon-limited measurement; series B suffers a positional disturbance partway through; series C is progressively photoreduced; series D is bumped once, late. Every plotted number is the output of the implementation cited in the footnote, not an illustration drawn by hand.

1The data

One scan repetition is one pass of the monochromator across the energy range, recording detector counts at each energy point. A series of n reps on one sample position gives a stack of n spectra measured on a common energy grid. Nothing below depends on how the absorption was detected – transmission, total fluorescence yield and high-energy-resolution fluorescence detection9 all produce the same object, a stack of spectra on a shared energy axis.

Each rep is edge-step normalised before anything else happens: the pre-edge background is subtracted and the spectrum is scaled so that the pre-edge region sits at 0 and the post-edge region at 1, making the absorption edge exactly one unit tall.1 Two consequences matter here. First, reps taken at different sample thicknesses or concentrations become directly comparable, so the stack can be averaged. Second – and this drives the whole of §2 – the flat regions of the spectrum now have near-zero dispersion by construction, not because they were measured well.

Figure 1. Six reps of an Fe K-edge spectrum on one sample position, after edge-step normalisation. The reps agree on the shape of the spectrum and disagree in the noise. Everything that follows is a way of quantifying that disagreement and deciding when it has become small enough.

2The feature window

Convergence is not a property of a whole spectrum; it is a property of the part of the spectrum a result depends on. The feature window [Emin, Emax] is the energy range bracketing that part – typically the white line, sometimes a pre-edge peak, an edge position, or a named EXAFS oscillation.

A window of roughly 10–20 eV, chosen to start a few eV below where the feature begins to rise and to end a few eV above where it returns to the local baseline, isolates the part of the spectrum that carries information.

The window has two jobs, and they disagree at its edges. It has to bracket the whole feature, shoulders included, because the per-rep value of §7 is usually an integral, and an integral truncated on the flanks of a peak is not that peak's area. It also serves as the averaging domain for a relative error, which needs a denominator that is not near zero – and on the shoulders the signal is at its weakest, so a relative error computed there is large for reasons that have nothing to do with the quality of the measurement. Narrowing the window would fix the statistics by mismeasuring the feature. Keeping the shoulders and excluding them from the averages fixes the statistics instead: the outermost 5% of points at each end, at least one point per end, are trimmed.

3The merged spectrum and its standard error

The merge of the first n reps is their point-by-point mean:

merged spectrum μn(E)  =  (1/n)  ∑i=1..n  yi(E)

where yi(E) is rep i at energy E. This is the spectrum that gets published. The question is how well it is known.

Standard deviation versus standard error

These are different quantities and the distinction is the core of everything below.

The sample standard deviation sn(E) measures how much the individual reps disagree with one another at energy E:

dispersion between reps, at one energy sn(E)2  =  (1/(n−1))  ∑i=1..n  [ yi(E) − μn(E) ]2

The divisor n−1 rather than n is Bessel's correction. An estimator is biased if its average value over many repeats of the experiment differs from the quantity it is estimating; unlike noise, bias does not average away. The deviations summed above are measured from the sample mean rather than from the true mean, and the sample mean sits closer to the data than the true mean does, so every squared deviation comes out systematically too small. Dividing by n−1 rather than n scales the sum back up by exactly the right amount and makes s2 an unbiased estimate of the true variance σ2. It also means sn is undefined at n = 1: a single scan contains no information about disagreement between scans.

sn does not shrink as reps accumulate. It is a property of the measurement, and its expected value is the same whether two reps or twenty have been taken. What shrinks is the uncertainty on the average – the standard error of the mean, or SEM:

standard error of the merge, at one energy SEMn(E)  =  sn(E)  /  [ c4(n) · √(n) ]

The √(n) is the reason repeating scans works at all: averaging n independent measurements of the same quantity produces an estimate that is √(n) times tighter than any one of them. The factor c4(n) corrects a small-sample bias in sn and is derived in §4.

{{FIG3}}

Figure 3. The merge of the first 2, 4 and 12 reps of series A over the feature window, each with its ±SEM band. The centre line barely moves; the band tightens by a factor of √(6) ≈ 2.4 between n = 2 and n = 12.

Reducing the window to a single number

SEMn(E) is a curve, one value per energy point, in the same units as the spectrum. To compare it against a target, against other samples, and against the physical limit defined in §5, it is converted to a relative error at each point – divided by the local signal μn(E) – and averaged over the trimmed window:

the convergence metric SEM(n)  =  100% ×  ⟨ sn(E)  /  [ c4(n) · √(n) · μn(E) ] ⟩E ∈ window

⟨ · ⟩E denotes the mean over the trimmed feature window. SEM(n) is a percentage, and it is undefined at n = 1.

Dividing by the local signal is what makes the number portable: a 1% relative error means the same thing on a dilute sample as on a concentrated one. It is also why the window matters so much. Outside the window, μn(E) → 0 in the pre-edge region, and a ratio with a vanishing denominator would dominate the average while carrying no information.

{{FIG4}}

Figure 4. How a stack becomes one number, for series A at n = 12. (a) The merge, with error bars showing ±s12(E) – the disagreement between the twelve thin traces behind it. (b) The same quantity divided by √(12)·c4 and by the local signal, giving a relative error at each energy. Its average over the untrimmed points is SEM(12) = 0.92%.

4The small-sample correction c4(n)

Bessel's correction removes the bias in s2. A second bias survives it, because averaging and taking a square root do not commute. The square root is concave, so the average of √(s2) falls below √(average of s2) – Jensen's inequality – and s is therefore a biased estimate of σ even though s2 is an unbiased estimate of σ2. For normally distributed data the shortfall has a closed form:2

expected value of the sample standard deviation E[s]  =  c4(n) · σ    where    c4(n)  =  √(2/(n−1))  ·  Γ(n/2) / Γ((n−1)/2)

Γ is the gamma function, the continuous extension of the factorial: Γ(k) = (k−1)! for integer k. The name c4 is the label this constant carries in the statistical process-control literature, where it is tabulated rather than computed.3

The bias is severe exactly where scan series live. The sample standard deviation reads 20% low at n = 2, 8% low at n = 4, and is still 2% low at n = 14. Dividing by c4(n) removes it. The correction is applied wherever a standard deviation is divided by √(n) in what follows, both to the spectrum-level curve SEM(n) of §3 and to the running standard error of the per-rep feature value in §7.

{{FIG5}}

Figure 5. (a) c4(n) against rep count. (b) The consequence for series A. The uncorrected curve is biased low by a known, reproducible amount – 20% at n = 2, 11% at n = 3 – which is enough to put the opening points of the curve underneath the counting-statistics floor of §5 and invite exactly the wrong reading of the plot. Dividing by c4(n) removes the offset and the curve sits on the floor, where a clean photon-limited measurement belongs.

5The counting-statistics floor

X-ray detection is photon counting. Photons arrive independently, so the number recorded in a fixed interval follows a Poisson distribution, whose defining property is that its variance equals its mean. Recording N photons therefore carries a standard deviation of √(N) and a relative error of

poisson relative error √(N) / N  =  1 / √(N)

Nothing in the analysis can improve on this. It is fixed by how many photons were recorded, and it is the reason count rate and integration time – not repetition count – are the levers that change what is achievable.4

Merging n reps multiplies the total recorded photons by n, so the achievable relative error on the merge falls as 1/√(n). Averaged over the same trimmed window:

counting-statistics floor at n reps floor(n)  =  100% ×  ⟨ 1 / √(N(E)) ⟩E ∈ window  /  √(n)

N(E) is the mean per-rep detector counts at energy E – the raw counts, before normalisation, summed across the reps collected and divided by their number.

The decisive property of floor(n) is that it is computed from the recorded counts and not from the dispersion between reps. It is an independent, absolute reference. That turns an unanswerable question – "is 0.92% a good number?" – into a readable one:

  • SEM(n) tracking floor(n) – the only thing limiting the measurement is the number of photons collected. More reps will help, as 1/√(n).
  • SEM(n) sitting above floor(n) – the dispersion between reps exceeds what photon statistics can account for. Something other than counting noise is contributing.
  • SEM(n) persistently well below floor(n) – the two quantities are describing different data, almost always because the counts came from a different detector channel than the spectrum. Note the word persistently: sn is itself an estimate from a handful of reps, so the ratio scatters about 1 by roughly 10% at small n, and individual points below 1 are ordinary. A whole series sitting below the floor is not.
What the comparison assumes. floor(n) is a relative error on the raw counts, while SEM(n) is a relative error on the edge-step normalised spectrum. Normalisation is affine, so propagating counting error through it gives a relative error on the normalised spectrum of [1 + b(E)/(Δμ·μn(E))]/√(N), where b(E) is the subtracted pre-edge background and Δμ the edge step. The bracket is always at least 1, so floor(n) as computed is a slight under-estimate of the true achievable error – a genuine lower bound, which is what a floor should be, and the reason a ratio sitting below 1 across a whole series indicates a mismatched input rather than a measurement that beat physics.
{{FIG6}}

Figure 6. Series A – a photon-limited measurement. (a) On log–log axes the 1/√(n) floor is a straight line of slope −½, and the measured SEM lies on it. (b) The same information as the ratio SEM(n)/floor(n), which stays between 0.95 and 1.05 at every rep count – variation about 1, not a trend away from it. The one point that falls below the floor, at n = 4, is the scatter of a standard deviation estimated from four reps rather than a measurement that beat physics. Nothing but photon count is limiting this measurement; the 1% target is met at rep 10.

6Departure from the floor

The rep count at which the measured curve leaves the floor and stays away from it is the quantity plateau_from_rep. It is located as follows. Let r(n) = SEM(n)/floor(n). A baseline r0 is taken as the median of the first three defined values of r, before something plausibly would have gone wrong. The onset is the smallest n for which

departure criterion r(n) > 1.25 r0   and   median{ r(m) : m ≥ n } > 1.25 r0

The second condition is what distinguishes a systematic from an outlier. A single anomalous rep lifts r at one point and the series returns; a real change in the measurement lifts r and it stays lifted. Requiring the median of everything from n onward to remain above the threshold selects only the second case. Locating the onset rather than merely noting that the series as a whole underperforms is the point of the exercise: the reps before the onset are still good data.

{{FIG7}}

Figure 7. Series B – the sample position was disturbed after rep 5. The ratio opens at 1.30 and settles to 1.10–1.16 over reps 3–5; from rep 6 it jumps to 1.97 and climbs to 2.49, staying above 1.9 for the remaining nine reps. That is a sustained departure rather than a fluctuation, so it is flagged: plateau_from_rep = 6. Reps 1–5 are a clean measurement; reps 6–14 are a measurement of something else.

What a departure does and does not mean

It is worth being precise here, because the natural reading is slightly wrong. SEM(n) continues to fall as 1/√(n) after the departure – visibly so in Figure 7. Additional reps do keep making the merged spectrum more precise. What has changed is what the merged spectrum is precise about.

Two situations produce a departure, and the ratio test cannot tell them apart:

  • Excess random dispersion – unstable incident flux, detector dead-time fluctuation, a beam spot wandering over a heterogeneous sample. The merge remains an estimate of a single quantity, but each rep is buying less than it should. The correct response is to fix the instability, not to pay √(n) for it.
  • A change in the sample or the instrument – a step in the optics, a spot move, beam damage. The merge is now an average over two or more distinct states, and it converges to a weighted mean that corresponds to none of them. Its SEM shrinks toward zero and describes nothing.

In both cases the merge has stopped being photon-limited, which is enough to justify stopping and diagnosing. Distinguishing them is the job of §7.

7Stationarity: testing whether the sample changed

A series is stationary if the quantity being measured holds still while it is measured, so that the reps vary about a fixed value. It is drifting if that quantity is systematically moving. At a beamline the usual causes are photoreduction or other beam damage pulling the white line down, the beam spot walking off the sample, monochromator energy calibration creeping (it is important to derive the energy axis using the monochromator encoder to avoid this), or changes in incident flux or sample temperature.5

This is the failure mode a precision test structurally cannot see. Averaging assumes every rep samples the same underlying quantity. If it is moving, SEM still falls as 1/√(n), and the analysis reports an ever more precise estimate of a number that corresponds to no actual state of the sample. Precision rises while meaning falls.

The per-rep feature value

The test operates not on the spectra but on a scalar summary of each rep. Each rep is reduced to one number xi over the same feature window – most usefully the integral of the window (peak area), but also its maximum (white-line height), its argmax (peak position), or its range (prominence above the local baseline). The result is a trace x1, …, xn in collection order, and the question becomes whether that trace has a trend.

The integral is generally the right choice for a white line. The maximum is an order statistic: it is upward-biased, and it deliberately selects the noisiest single point in the window, inflating rep-to-rep dispersion for no gain in sensitivity.

Mann–Kendall: is there a trend?

For every pair of reps i < j, record only whether the later value is higher or lower than the earlier one. A pair is concordant if the later value is higher and discordant if it is lower. Kendall's rank correlation coefficient τ is the normalised difference between the two counts:6

kendall rank correlation τ  =  (C − D) / [ n(n−1)/2 ]      τ ∈ [−1, +1]

C and D are the numbers of concordant and discordant pairs and n(n−1)/2 is the total number of pairs. τ = +1 means every rep exceeded every rep before it; τ = −1 means strictly falling; τ = 0 means no monotone tendency.

The associated p-value is the probability of observing a τ at least this far from zero if the reps had been in random order. A small p-value means the ordering is unlikely to be an accident. The null distribution needed to turn τ into a probability comes from the orderings themselves: if rep order carries no information then all n! permutations are equally likely, and the fraction of them reaching a given |τ| can be counted exactly. At the rep counts of interest here that exact enumeration is what is used. For larger n it is replaced by a normal approximation, in which the concordance excess C − D has zero mean and variance n(n−1)(2n+5)/18 under the null. The test is distribution-free: it assumes nothing about the shape of the noise and does not require the drift to be linear. Because it uses only the direction of each pairwise comparison and not the magnitude, one wild rep cannot manufacture a trend. It needs at least four reps to have any power; below that the correct output is unknown, not no trend.

Theil–Sen: how large is it?

Mann–Kendall reports whether a trend is real, not whether it matters. The magnitude comes from the Theil–Sen estimator, the median of the slopes of all lines through pairs of points:7

robust slope, per rep slope  =  mediani < j  ( xj − xi ) / ( j − i )

Reported as a fraction of the mean, and multiplied by n−1, it gives the total excursion: how far the feature value moved across the whole series, as a percentage.

The estimator is chosen for its breakdown point – the fraction of the data that can be arbitrarily corrupted before the estimate becomes meaningless. For Theil–Sen it is about 29%: roughly one rep in three can be garbage. Ordinary least squares has a breakdown point of zero; a single outlier drags the fitted slope arbitrarily far.8

Why both are required

Significance without magnitude is a trap in the other direction. Given enough reps, Mann–Kendall resolves arbitrarily small trends, so a real but scientifically irrelevant 0.1% drift would eventually block a perfectly good merge. The series is therefore called drifting only when the trend is both significant and material:

drift criterion drifting  ⇔  ( p < 0.05 )  and  ( total excursion > 1% of the mean )

The 1% is deliberately the same scale as the SEM target: a trend smaller than the noise level being demanded is not a trend worth stopping for.

{{FIG8}}

Figure 8. Per-rep white-line area, as a percentage of the series mean, with the Theil–Sen line. (a) Series A varies about a fixed value; τ = −0.06 is not significant (p = 0.84) and the implied excursion is 0.23%, below the materiality threshold. (b) Series C is being photoreduced: τ = −0.55, p = 0.014, total excursion 2.1%. Both conditions are met, so the series is called drifting.

The complementarity is the point. Series C tracks the counting floor for its whole length – SEM(n)/floor(n) stays between 1.05 and 1.18, never approaching the 25% departure threshold, and the merge meets its 1% target at rep 12. On the §5 test alone it is a textbook photon-limited measurement. It is also a sample that lost 2% of its white-line area to the beam while it was being measured. Conversely, series B is caught cleanly by the floor test (Figure 7) and passes the drift test with p = 0.52, because a single step change is not a monotone trend. Neither test subsumes the other.

The rising-SEM veto

One check requires no statistical test at all. For genuine repetitions of one measurement the running standard error can only fall: s converges to a fixed σ while the divisor √(n) grows. If the running SEM grows instead, σ is being re-estimated upward faster than √(n) shrinks it, which happens only when later reps disagree with earlier ones.

It is treated as an automatic veto on a converged verdict. The test compares the last value of the running SEM against the smallest value in the final third of the series, requiring at least six reps and a rise of more than 25% before firing – comparing against a window minimum rather than the immediately preceding point so that one noisy rep does not trip it.

{{FIG9}}

Figure 9. (a) Running SEM of the feature value for series A (clean) and series C (photoreduced). Neither comes within a factor of three of the 1% target at any rep count: a threshold on precision alone passes both, and carries no information about which is a valid measurement. (b) Series D, disturbed once at rep 10. Mann–Kendall finds nothing here (τ = +0.06, p = 0.84) because a single late step is not monotone – but the running SEM turns back up, which repeats of one measurement cannot do, and the veto fires.

8Two further checks on the stack

Between-position heterogeneity

When reps come from more than one position on the sample, the stack may be averaging over genuinely different material rather than repeating one measurement. A one-way analysis of variance separates the two. At each energy point, the variance between position means is compared against the variance within positions:

heterogeneity f-statistic, at one energy F(E)  =  [ ∑k nk (μk(E) − μ(E))2 / (K−1) ]  /  [ ∑k ∑i (yki(E) − μk(E))2 / (n − K) ]

K is the number of positions, nk the rep count at position k, μk its mean spectrum, and μ the rep-weighted grand mean. The reported statistic is F(E) averaged over the window.

F ≈ 1 means positions differ by no more than reps at one position do, and the stack can be averaged. F > 5 means they differ well beyond shot noise: the combined average is a population mean over distinct chemistries, not the spectrum of one, and no number of additional reps will converge it to a single answer. Because each rep was edge-step normalised, differences in thickness or concentration between positions have already been absorbed – what remains is a difference in spectral shape.

Outlier screening, and a caution

Each rep can be compared against the merge by cosine similarity, the normalised inner product of the two spectra treated as vectors:

cosine similarity cos θ  =  ( a · b ) / ( ‖a‖ · ‖b‖ )

A rep scoring below 0.95 against the merge is worth looking at. Beyond that, the metric can be dominated by the overall amplitude of the vectors rather than by the differences between them: across the four series used in this document – including series B, where the sample position was lost, and series D, where the measurement was disturbed – the lowest similarity of any individual rep to the merge is 0.9993, and the cumulative-average similarity is at least 0.999995 in every case, making it difficult to use as a stopping criterion.

9Projecting the cost of finishing

When a series is stationary, photon-limited, and still short of its target, the remaining question is arithmetic. SEM falls as k/√(n), so the total rep count needed to reach a target T scales as the square of how far away it currently is:

projected total reps to reach the target nrequired  =  ⌈ n · ( SEM(n) / T )2 ⌉

The square is what makes this worth computing rather than guessing. Halving the remaining error costs four times the beam time. A series at 1.4% with a 1% target needs twice its current rep count; the same series with a 0.5% target needs eight times.

{{FIG10}}

Figure 10. Series A, projected forward from rep 5. At that point SEM = 1.39%, giving nrequired = ⌈5 × (1.39/1.00)2⌉ = 10. The series in fact met the target at rep 10.

This number, not the current SEM, is what should be weighed against the remaining time. If ten reps are needed and there is time for four, the available levers are longer integration per point, fewer filters or a higher incident flux, or an explicitly relaxed target – not more reps of the same length, which is the one option the arithmetic has already ruled out.

10The decision, in order

After each rep, three questions. The order is not cosmetic: questions 1 and 2 identify the cases in which additional data is worthless, and question 3 is the only one whose answer can ever be keep collecting. Asking 3 first will sign off a sample that is being destroyed, because a destroyed sample passes it.

QuestionCriterionIf yes
1. Is it still the same sample? Mann–Kendall p < 0.05 and Theil–Sen total excursion > 1%; or the running SEM has risen by more than 25% off its recent minimum Stop and diagnose. More reps sharpen a moving target. Check for beam damage and spot drift.
2. Is it still photon-limited? SEM(n)/floor(n) has risen 25% above its opening value and stayed there – plateau_from_rep is set Stop and diagnose. The dispersion exceeds what photon statistics can account for. The reps before the onset are the clean data.
3. Is the precision sufficient? SEM(n) ≤ the target (1% by default) Stop. The requested statistics have been achieved; further reps buy precision nothing downstream can use.
If none of the above On the floor, stationary, still above target Continue. Reps still buy signal-to-noise as 1/√(n); compare nrequired against the time remaining.

A fifth outcome sits outside the sequence: if the positions being averaged are chemically heterogeneous (F > 5), the series has no single answer to converge to, and the choice is between reporting a population average and analysing the positions separately.
